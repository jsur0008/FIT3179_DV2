HORROR IS GROWING

Horror movies is a genre that often explores dark subject matter and seeks to elicit physical or psychological fear in its viewers. Horror films have existed since the early 20th century where elements of horror can be found in media predating film, including folklore, mythology, and literature leading up to its birth.

Even though, due to their subject matter, horror has always had some stigma attached to it, in recent years there seems to be a surge of horror movies in mainstream media.

The number of horror films listed on TMDB has grown from a handful in the early 1900s to about 28,000 in the 2020s so far. The rise is steepest from 2000: about 8,200 films in the 2000s, 19,500 in the 2010s and 28,000 in the 2020s, with years still to run.

This is not only because more films are made overall. Horror's share of all films has risen too, from about 1 in every 100 in the 1890s to 5 in every 100 in the 1980s and 9 in every 100 in the 2020s.

Among genres, horror ranked 17th in the 1940s, rose to 8th in the 1980s and reached 4th in the 2010s. It holds that place in the 2020s, behind only Drama, Documentary and Comedy.

Horror is made all over the world, but it is concentrated in a few countries. The United States has by far the largest circle, with more than 30,000 horror films listed, followed by countries such as the United Kingdom, Japan, Canada and France. Smaller circles appear across Europe, Asia, Latin America and Australia.

However, early horror belonged to Japan. In the 1910s, Japanese studios were listed with 102 horror films, ahead of the United States with 70. Japan was still leading in some single years as late as 1938. By the 1920s, though, Hollywood had taken over, and the United States has topped every decade since.

CHEAP TO MAKE, BIG PAYOFF

Horror is one of the cheapest genres to make. A scary film needs a few actors, a dark house and a good idea, not expensive stars or special effects. That keeps the risk low, and it is why studios keep making them. Most horror films earn modest amounts, but a few earn many times what they cost.

The big payoffs belong to two specialists. A typical Blumhouse film cost about $9 million and took about $91 million, a multiple of 10. Atomic Monster, the company behind The Conjuring films, goes further, turning about $18 million into about $206 million (a multiple of 11.5).

The major studios, such as Universal, Paramount, Warner Bros. and Columbia, sit at roughly 2 to 3 times, and some of the old names (Miramax, MGM) barely clear 1.1.

Three franchises (The Conjuring Universe, Alien and Resident Evil) earn nearly a third of the $20.8 billion made by the 20 biggest horror series.

The money is mostly recent. The Conjuring Universe, It, A Quiet Place and Five Nights at Freddy's are made up almost entirely of films from the 2010s and 2020s. Because the figures are not adjusted for inflation, older hits like Jaws and The Exorcist look smaller than they were at the time.

WHERE IT'S FILMED AND WHO MAKES IT

Horror stories are often set in one place and shot in another. This act follows horror from the page to the set to the studio. It looks at where stories are set compared with where they are filmed, which places attract the cameras, and which companies are behind the films.

Films often travel to shoot. The most common route is stories set in the United States and filmed in Canada, with 104 routes, followed by the United States and the United Kingdom with 78. Stories set in the United Kingdom are often filmed in the Czech Republic (20 routes).

Shooting abroad can be cheaper, and some countries offer filmmakers tax incentives. A forest, castle or old city in one country can also stand in for a setting somewhere else. The favourite destination changes by decade: Canada leads in the 2000s (56 routes), but the United Kingdom takes over in the 2010s (45, against 16 for Canada) and stays ahead in the 2020s.

Horror shoots in a few hotspots. Of the 124 squares on the map that were ever used, just 32 hold about 85% of all filming locations. The biggest are around Los Angeles and the rest of California (together about a quarter of the total), then New York, Vancouver and Toronto, and the London area, home of Pinewood Studios.

The hotspots are moving. North America's share of filming locations falls from 82% in the 1980s to 56% in the 2020s, while Europe's doubles from 16% to 32%. Vancouver peaks in the 2000s with 10% of the decade's locations, and Paris and New Orleans join the busiest squares in the 2020s. The 2020s have fewer records so far because the decade is not over, so treat that decade's shares with care.

Horror has no single home. Of the 12 largest companies, seven are American, three are Japanese, and one each is British and Filipino. Universal Pictures leads with 209 horror films, just ahead of Japan's Amumo 98 with 200.

The shared scale shows two ways of making horror. Older studios spread their films over decades: on the five-year average, Universal never passes about 7 films a year, and Hammer, Toei, TOHO and Paramount stay below 5. Newer companies work in bursts. Amumo 98 made all 200 of its films in 17 years and peaks at about 19 a year. Brain Damage Films peaks at about 16, with 152 of its 159 films since 2000. Blumhouse and Amumo 98 both started in 2009.

HORROR SUBGENRES

Horror is not just one thing. We sorted films into the 23 subgenres Blumhouse describes, using TMDB keywords. Only around 45% of films have keywords, and 71% of those fit at least one subgenre.

Horror's most common subgenre has changed over time. Sci-fi horror made up 39% of tagged films in the 1950s, the biggest share of any cell on the chart, with creature features close behind at 29%. Both have faded, to 13% and 11% in the 2020s. Supernatural horror is the steady one, holding between 16% and 24% in every decade. Horror comedy has gone the other way, from 6% in the 1950s to around 19% today.

Some subgenres belong to one era. Slashers peak in the 1980s at 14%. Found footage was almost absent until the 2000s. It had a few early examples, but the style only became common after The Blair Witch Project (1999), a very cheap film that became a huge hit. It then jumps to 19% in the 2010s and falls back to 11% in the 2020s.

OVERLOOKED FROM THE SPOTLIGHT

Horror is one of cinema's most popular genres, but awards have been slow to follow. The Academy Awards have nominated horror far less often than its share of films would suggest. The first chart compares the genre's share of all films with its share of Oscar nominations, and the second looks at the horror films the Academy has nominated.

The gap is widest in the 2000s and 2010s. In the 2010s, horror made up about 7 in every 100 films but only about 1 in every 100 Oscar nominations. From the 1950s on, its share of nominations is below its share of films in every decade. Horror is a growing share of what gets made and a reliable earner, yet the industry still does not reward it in proportion.

Horror has long been underrepresented at the Oscars, and most nominated films received only one or two nominations. But 2025 was a turning point. Sinners received 16 nominations, the most of any film in Academy history, and lifted horror's share of nominations in the 2020s. However, the gap has not closed. Horror still gets about 5 of every 100 nominations while making up about 9 of every 100 films. Whether this marks a lasting change is still open, since the 2020s are not over yet.

HORROR IN AUSTRALIA

Films must be classified before they can be shown in Australian cinemas, and horror lands mostly in the two strongest everyday categories. This act looks at how Australia rates horror, when horror arrives, and where you can see it on a big screen.

Of the 1,424 horror films in this chart that have an Australian classification, 78% are rated M or MA15+, and that rises to 82% from 2000 on. In the late 1970s and 1980s, by comparison, 40% of classified films were R18+ or refused classification (RC).

MA15+ took over in the mid-1990s, going from 12% of films in 1990–94 to 52% in 1995–99, and it has stayed above half ever since. The chart also widens sharply from the mid-2000s, with 467 films in 2020–24 against 80 in 2000–04. Almost no horror films are rated G or PG (5% combined). The 17 refused classification, mostly from the late 1970s and 1980s, cannot legally be sold, hired or shown in Australia.

October is the busiest month for horror releases in Australia, with 176 of the 1,396 films (12.6%), almost double the quietest months of March (6.4%) and December (6.7%). Releases build through the second half of the year, from 9.2% in September to 10.4% in November, so October and November together bring 23% of the year's horror films. If releases were spread evenly, every month would have about 8.3%. The likely reason is Halloween on 31 October: it is the time of year when audiences are most in the mood to be scared, so distributors aim to have their horror films in cinemas just before it.

Cinemas are concentrated along the east and south-east coast, where most Australians live, with dense clusters around Brisbane, Sydney, Melbourne and Adelaide and a smaller cluster around Perth. Across the interior and the north there are only scattered dots, so a big-screen horror film is a long drive away for much of the country.

WHERE HORROR IS LEADING

Horror's rise is not a passing spike. It has gone from about 1 in every 100 films to about 9. Cheap budgets keep the risk low, and a few films earn 10 times their budget or more, so studios have every reason to keep making it. New companies such as Blumhouse and Amumo 98 only started in 2009, and the favourite place to film has already moved from Canada to the United Kingdom.

The next wave may look different, because tastes change quickly: found footage jumped to 19% of tagged films in the 2010s and fell back to 11%. Recognition may be catching up, slowly. Sinners set a record with 16 Oscar nominations, but horror still gets about 5 in every 100 nominations for 9 in every 100 films. In Australia, expect more of the same: mostly M and MA15+ films, released around Halloween.

Method notes

  • The two data sources combined are TMDB (films) and the Oscar dataset (awards), with Wikipedia, OpenStreetMap/Overture and Natural Earth for franchises, cinemas and map shapes.
  • A film with several countries of origin counts once for each country.
  • Subgenres are matched from TMDB keywords. Only some films have keywords, so subgenre shares describe the tagged films only, and a film can count in more than one subgenre.
  • Revenue and budget are in US dollars and are not adjusted for inflation. Only films with both figures recorded are used for budget and revenue.
  • TMDB lists production companies, not distributors, so companies that mainly distribute can look smaller than they are.
  • Story and filming locations come from Wikidata, so they cover only films that have those details recorded there. Films were matched to TMDB by [IMDb ID / title and year, whichever you used].
  • Where a location is a whole country, its point sits at the middle of the country.
  • "Typical" film means the median film. A company's multiple is its median revenue divided by its median budget.
  • Revenue is worldwide box office. It is not profit, because cinemas take a share and marketing costs are not in the budget.
  • 2026 is not a complete year, so figures for the 2020s will rise.
