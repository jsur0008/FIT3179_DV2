HORROR IS GROWING

Horror movies is a genre that often explores dark subject matter and seeks to elicit physical or psychological fear in its viewers. Horror films have existed since the early 20th century where elements of horror can be found in media predating film, including folklore, mythology, and literature leading up to its birth.

Even though, due to their subject matter, horror has always had some stigma attached to it, in recent years there seems to be a surge of horror movies in mainstream media.

The number of horror films listed on TMDB has grown from a handful in the early 1900s to about 28,000 in the 2020s so far. The rise is steepest from 2000: about 8,200 films in the 2000s, 19,500 in the 2010s and 28,000 in the 2020s, with years still to run.

This is not only because more films are made overall. Horror's share of all films has risen too, from about 1 in every 100 in the 1890s to 5 in every 100 in the 1980s and 9 in every 100 in the 2020s.

Among genres, horror ranked 17th in the 1940s, rose to 8th in the 1980s and reached 4th in the 2010s. It holds that place in the 2020s, behind only Drama, Documentary and Comedy.

Horror is made all over the world, but it is concentrated in a few countries. The United States has by far the largest circle, with more than 30,000 horror films listed, followed by countries such as the United Kingdom, Japan, Canada and France. Smaller circles appear across Europe, Asia, Latin America and Australia.

However, early horror belonged to Japan. In the 1910s, Japanese studios were listed with 102 horror films, ahead of the United States with 70. Japan was still leading in some single years as late as 1938. By the 1920s, though, Hollywood had taken over, and the United States has topped every decade since.

CHEAP TO MAKE, BIG PAYOFF

Horror is one of the cheapest genres to make. A scary film needs a few actors, a dark house and a good idea, not expensive stars or special effects. That keeps the risk low, and it is why studios keep making them. Most horror films earn modest amounts, but a few earn many times what they cost.

5Budget against revenue

Each row is a production company. The grey dot is the budget of its typical horror film and the red dot is the revenue. The scale is logarithmic, so equal steps along the axis mean the same multiple, not the same number of dollars. The longer the line, the more a typical film earns back; the number at the end is that multiple.

The big payoffs belong to two specialists. A typical Blumhouse film cost about $9 million and took about $91 million, a multiple of 10. Atomic Monster, the company behind The Conjuring films, goes further, turning about $18 million into about $206 million (a multiple of 11.5).

The major studios, such as Universal, Paramount, Warner Bros. and Columbia, sit at roughly 2 to 3 times, and some of the old names (Miramax, MGM) barely clear 1.1.

6The biggest horror franchises

Each bar is one franchise, and its length is the total worldwide box office. Each segment is one film, oldest on the left, coloured by the decade it was released.

Three franchises (The Conjuring Universe, Alien and Resident Evil) earn nearly a third of the $20.8 billion made by the 20 biggest horror series.

The money is mostly recent. The Conjuring Universe, It, A Quiet Place and Five Nights at Freddy's are made up almost entirely of films from the 2010s and 2020s. Because the figures are not adjusted for inflation, older hits like Jaws and The Exorcist look smaller than they were at the time.

Subgenres

Horror is not just one thing. We sorted films into the 23 subgenres Blumhouse describes, using TMDB keywords. Only around 45% of films have keywords, and 71% of those fit at least one subgenre.

Horror’s most common subgenre has changed over time. Sci-fi horror made up 39% of tagged films in the 1950s, the biggest share of any cell on the chart, with creature features close behind at 29%. Both have faded, to 13% and 11% in the 2020s. Supernatural horror is the steady one, holding between 16% and 24% in every decade. Horror comedy has gone the other way, from 6% in the 1950s to around 19% today.

Some subgenres belong to one era. Slashers peak in the 1980s at 14%. Found footage was almost absent until the 2000s, because it had to wait for cheap, portable cameras, with The Blair Witch Project (1999), jumpstarting its popularity. It then jumps to 19% in the 2010s and falls back to 11% in the 2020s.

HORROR IN AUSTRALIA

[How Australia rates horror, when horror arrives, and where you can see it on a big screen.]

October is the busiest month for horror releases in Australia, with 176 of the 1,396 films (12.6%), almost double the quietest months of March (6.4%) and December (6.7%). Releases build through the second half of the year, with September at 9.2% and November at 10.4%, so October and November together account for 23% of the year’s horror releases. If horror films were spread evenly, every month would have about 8.3%.

Cinemas are concentrated along the east and south-east coast, where most Australians live, with dense clusters around Brisbane, Sydney, Melbourne and Adelaide, and a smaller cluster around Perth. Across the interior and the north there are only scattered dots, so a big-screen horror film is a long drive away for much of the country.
